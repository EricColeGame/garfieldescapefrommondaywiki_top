### Why the Franchise Fits a Video Game

Garfield has been a multimedia property for decades, moving from newspaper pages to television specials, feature films, and merchandise. Created by Jim Davis and published through [the official Garfield website](https://www.garfield.com/), the character has stayed recognizable because his personality never really changed: lazy, sarcastic, food-motivated, and deeply committed to doing as little as possible.

That's a tricky protagonist to design around. A hero who hates effort doesn't naturally fit a high-speed action game. The smartest licensed games solve this by making the laziness itself the mechanic — you're not running because you want to, you're running because Monday is chasing you.

### What to Look For in the Mechanics

A licensed platformer lives or dies on feel, and there are three things worth checking the moment hands-on impressions land. First, jump timing — if the character has weight and momentum, the game respects its source material. Second, checkpointing — family games should forgive mistakes, not punish them. Third, collectibles. Lasagna as currency is an obvious choice, and if the game uses it to unlock cosmetics or bonus stages, that's a good sign the developers understood the assignment.

Player experience with licensed games also suggests watching for repetition. If every level is the same obstacle course with a different backdrop, the Monday joke wears thin fast.
